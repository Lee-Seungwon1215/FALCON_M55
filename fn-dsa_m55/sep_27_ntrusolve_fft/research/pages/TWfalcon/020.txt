all TW functions are optimized in assembly for the ARMv7-M architecture, as used by
the Cortex-M4F, and fully constant-time based on the rules discussed in Section 4.3.
The benchmarks are performed by directly calling the assembly functions and measuring
the number of clock cycles required for execution. This is achieved by reusing the
benchmarking done by [Por25a] in combination with the benchmark framework in the
PQM4 library. In summary, the cycle counter is enabled, the CPU frequency is set to 20
MHz, and the CPU cycle counter is read before and after each operation to compute the
cycle count.
Results in Table 5 show that TW arithmetic is generally slower than integer-emulated
floating-point arithmetic on the Cortex-M4. The core reason is that TW implementations
rely, besides for some constant-time modifications, exclusively on the FPU, which provides
only a small set of basic operations: addition, multiplication, division, square root, and
FMA. Each of these has non-negligible latency, for example, FMA requires three cycles,
and if its output is used immediately by the next instruction, an additional cycle is
required. This pipeline sensitivity is common in TW code, where sequences of dependent
multiplications and additions are unavoidable. Moreover, enforcing constant-time execution
amplifies these costs: conditional branches must be replaced with masking operations,
which require moving values between integer and floating-point registers at a cost of one
cycle per move, and in many cases intermediate results must be saved to and reloaded from
the stack. These factors accumulate in simple yet frequent operations such as addition
and add/sub, where TW ends up several times slower.
By contrast, integer-emulated arithmetic can take advantage of the ARM ALU and DSP
instruction set, which is far richer and better optimized for multi-word integer arithmetic.
Instructions such as UMLAL perform 32 ×32 →64 multiplication with accumulation in a
single cycle. In addition, shifts can be fused into arithmetic instructions at no extra cost,
andconditionalmovescanberealizedusingtheSEL instruction, thoughthiscanalsobeused
in the constant-time Merge algorithm for TW. These capabilities allow integer-emulated
implementations to align mantissas, perform multi-word additions/multiplications, and
implement conditional swaps at very low cost, while there is also no cycle penalty when the
output of an instruction is used by the next one, like for the FPU. As a result, the frequent
basic operations needed in Falcon, such as addition, subtraction, and multiplication, are
consistently faster in the integer-emulated implementation, despite the apparent simplicity
of the TW algorithms when expressed at the mathematical level.
While TW arithmetic is disadvantaged for frequent basic operations, it gains an
advantage in more complex functions such as reciprocal, division, and square root. In the
integer-emulated implementation, these operations require iterative bit-by-bit methods over
multi-word integers, leading to hundreds of cycles of work. In contrast, the TW versions
use a Newton–Raphson iteration approach, which results in a simpler algorithm that can
use the single-precision square root and division operation, and use TW multiplication and
FMA for other parts of the algorithm. As a result, TW implementations of division and
square root are several times faster than the corresponding integer-emulated algorithms,
even though they are slower for addition and multiplication.
