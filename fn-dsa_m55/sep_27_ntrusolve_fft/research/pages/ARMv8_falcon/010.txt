Figure 2 shows the proposed memory optimization path of
FFT/NTT-512 and FFT/NTT-1024. FFT is divided into each
Real and Image Part for the polynomial to be transformed,
and each FFT is performed. For example, FFT-512 performs
each transformation of a 256 degree of polynomial in the Real
Part and a 256 degree of polynomial in the Image Part. Thus,
in FFT-512 and FFT-1024, FFT depth for 256 degree of poly-
nomial and 512 degree of polynomial should be considered.
In FFT-512, from 256 degree of polynomial transformation
to 16 degree of polynomial transformation, it reaches with
only two merging. After that, memory access is not required
until the FFT is completed by applying the register holding
technique. In FFT-1024, the depth is odd, so it is impossible
to reach the register holding depth by utilizing merging tech-
nique only. Therefore, we added a standard implementation
technique to reach register holding depth. Because the NTT is
performed in the integer domain, it is not split into two parts
for polynomials like the FFT. NTT-512 and NTT-1024 per-
form transformation of polynomial degree supported by each
security level. Since depth is odd in NTT-512, like FFT-1024,
standard implementation is added to suggest optimal memory
path. In NTT-1024, it reaches register holding with only
3 merging. Through the proposed memory optimization path,
we further accelerated performance of FFT/NTT by mini-
mizing memory access instructions such as LD1 and ST1
instructions.

tion technique applied to Falcon’s FFT/NTT. Each point in
Figure 2 represents a Falcon’s polynomial of the 64th degree,

our FFT/NTT implementation. The black lines are the but-
terflies that are first-order realized in the merging technique.
